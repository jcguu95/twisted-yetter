\subsection{Direct-evaluator cost estimates}
\label{app:computational-cost}

We probed a historical direct sectorwise evaluator with the elementary
category $SU(2)_2$ and the familiar simply connected manifold $E(2)=K3$.
The diagram was a draft transcription of the $n=2$ specialization of
Gompf--Stipsicz Figure~8.15 \cite[Figure~8.15]{GompfStipsicz1999}.  It has $22$
ordinary components and therefore
$3^{22}=31{,}381{,}059{,}609$ simple-label sectors.  The transcription passed
recorded link-level checks but was not certified against the printed figure,
and no $E(2)$ invariant was completed.

An early informal extrapolation was roughly $350$ serial years, but we do not
use that number: no current reproducible artifact records its assumptions.
The retained record is
\nolinkurl{experiments/results/gompf-stipsicz/figure815-k3-e2-su2-2-optimistic-runtime-floor-v1.json}.
It labels itself
\texttt{HEURISTIC\_OPTIMISTIC\_RUNTIME\_FLOOR\_NOT\_RIGOROUS\_BOUND}; it also
records the hashes of its generator and probe, but was generated in a dirty
worktree.  Its deliberately favourable toy model extrapolates sampled fast
strata, charges fast sectors zero time, and charges every remaining sector
exactly $0.01$ seconds.  The resulting estimate is
$313{,}809{,}868.7$ worker-seconds, or $87{,}169.4$ worker-hours and
$9.944$ worker-years.  It gives $30.27$ days on $120$ perfectly utilized
workers.  Ideal linear scaling would require approximately $3{,}633$ workers
for one day, $87{,}170$ for one hour, or $5.23$ million for one minute.  In the
formal limit of one worker per sector, the toy model reaches $0.01$ seconds
only by using approximately $31.38$ billion workers and ignoring scheduling,
communication, memory, and the hard tail.  Thus the outer sum is embarrassingly
parallel, but the contraction inside a hard sector is not automatically so.

For a reproducible cloud-price scale, consider Hetzner's European CCX43
instance, listed with $16$ dedicated AMD vCPUs and $64$ GB RAM
\cite{HetznerCloudGeneralPurpose2026}.  Hetzner's price schedule effective
June~15, 2026 lists €0.4423 per hour and a €275.99 monthly cap, excluding VAT
and IPv4 \cite{HetznerCloudPricing2026}.  Identifying one perfectly utilized
worker with one dedicated vCPU converts the optimistic work volume into
$5{,}448.1$ CCX43-hours.  Eight fully utilized instances ($128$ workers) would
finish the toy workload in $28.38$ days.  The uncapped hourly arithmetic is
€2,409.69; if each instance reaches one monthly cap without entering a second
billing period, the corresponding capped arithmetic is €2,207.92.  Hetzner
actually bills the lesser of hourly usage and the applicable monthly cap for
each server \cite{HetznerCloudBilling2026}, so calendar placement matters.
These figures exclude VAT, IPv4, storage, excess traffic, orchestration,
failures, imperfect scaling, and the unmeasured hard tail.  They are therefore
pricing illustrations for the optimistic toy model, not quotes or upper bounds
for completing the computation.

A quantum computer does not presently turn this into a defensible runtime
estimate.  If every sector contribution could be implemented as a bounded,
coherent unit-cost oracle and only an additive approximation were required,
quantum amplitude estimation could give its usual quadratic query advantage
\cite{BrassardEtAl2002}.  Our contractions do not supply such an oracle for
free, and exact summation is not covered by that statement.  In particular,
replacing $3^{22}$ by its square root $3^{11}=177{,}147$ is not a valid
runtime prediction for the present evaluator.  A quantum estimate would
first require a reversible circuit for one sector, an error target, and a
fault-tolerant gate-cost analysis.
